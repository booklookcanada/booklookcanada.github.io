How to read this site

A figure looks wrong? — section 9 explains how disputes work. It is linked from the foot of every page and from every notice where a figure is held back.

1. The promise, and the rules we keep

BookLook turns the annual returns Canadian charities file with the Canada Revenue Agency into pages anyone can read: who paid for a charity's work, and how the charity reported spending the money. The rules:

  • As filed, always. Every figure comes from a charity's own return. We never adjust, estimate, or fill in a figure. A line a charity did not file is shown as not filed.
  • No scores, no grades, no verdicts. A page lays out what a return says. It never tells you whether a charity is good.
  • No rankings by ratio. Nothing on this site sorts charities by percentages. Size is shown in dollars; efficiency is never turned into a league table.
  • Held back beats guessed. Where a figure would mislead — because a return's totals don't add up, or a number is too small to read fairly — the page says so and explains why, instead of printing the figure.
  • Corrections are public. If we get something wrong, the fix and its date go in the change log (section 10).

2. Where the numbers come from

The Canada Revenue Agency publishes the financial parts of charities' T3010 returns as open data. This preview was built from those published files — filing years 2009 to 2024 — downloaded on October 29, 2026. It covers 39,699 Ontario charities and 487,398 returns.

Two things about the source matter. First, the CRA does not check these returns before publishing them; a figure here is what a charity told the CRA. Second, the published data lags: returns are due six months after a charity's year-end, and the newest filing year published is 2024, so the freshest page on this site rests on a return whose year ended about 22 months ago. That lag is the CRA's publication schedule, not any charity's lateness — every page prints its data age so you can see it.

For the 200 largest Ontario charities we also checked the websites the CRA lists for them: 129 of the 200 could be confirmed as the charity's own site, and 70 of those link from their own pages to a donation page — those, and only those, carry a Donate button here. Separately, we read the audited statements or annual reports that the largest charities post on their own websites and set them beside their returns: for 6 of the largest 200 we could extract comparable totals at all — most published statements are not machine-readable with any reliability. Paired with the return for the same fiscal year, the published figures sit within about 1.1% of the filed ones in every case where both exist; two of the six statements cover a year the CRA's data has not reached yet, so no comparison exists for them. A difference, where one exists, is logged — never quietly fixed.

3. What a return is

Every registered charity in Canada must file a T3010 return with the CRA each year, covering its fiscal year — which the charity chooses, so year-ends fall all over the calendar. The return reports money in (donations, government funding, sales), money out (its own programs, grants to other organizations, administration, fundraising), what it owns and owes, and what it pays people.

There are two shapes of return. Larger charities file the full financial schedule (Schedule 6) with every line. Smaller ones may file a short form (Section D) that does not ask for every line — it has no fundraising-cost line at all, for example. Pages say which shape a charity filed, and short-form pages show dollars instead of breakdowns the form never asked for.

4. How to read a page

Every page runs in the same order:

  • Standing — its newest return in the published data, how old that data is, and whether the return's parts add up to its totals. If a charity is in trouble with its filing, it shows here first.
  • Where its spending went — the split of its spending: what it spent on its own programs, and what it gave to other organizations, as two separate figures. They are never added together — a dollar granted onward is not the same fact as a dollar spent in-house.
  • What it costs to raise a dollar — where it can fairly be shown (section 6 explains when it isn't).
  • Where its money came from, what it holds, what it pays people, and what it says it does — in its own words, labelled as filed.
  • The last five years — its filing trail, with missing years printed as missing.
  • Gifts between organizations — who it gave to, and who gave to it (section 7).
  • What this page can't tell you — the limits, on every page.

Three pages that show the range: Daily Bread Food Bank — a large charity whose page shows the full per-$100 split. Mastercard Foundation — a foundation, whose page leads with what it holds and grants. MAIMONIDES SCHOOLS FOR JEWISH STUDIES — a small charity, whose page is written in dollars, not percentages.

5. Every figure, defined

Figures are per $100 of total spending (line 5100 of the return) unless stated otherwise.

  • Spent on its own programs — line 5000 (charitable activities it runs itself) ÷ line 5100 × 100.
  • Gave to other organizations — line 5050 (gifts to qualified donees) ÷ line 5100 × 100. Shown separately from its own programs, never added to them.
  • Fundraising — line 5020 ÷ line 5100 × 100. Administration — line 5010 ÷ line 5100 × 100. Political activities — line 5030, shown only when a charity filed more than $0. Everything else — whatever of line 5100 is left.
  • What it costs to raise a dollar — fundraising costs (line 5020) pooled over up to its three most recent usable years, ÷ cash donations over the same years: donations with receipts (line 4500) plus donations without (line 4530), minus gifts of goods where the CRA publishes that split (Schedule 5, most recent year), and excluding gifts from other charities and government money. Pooling keeps one unusual campaign from defining a charity.
  • Cash on hand, in months — cash and short-term investments (line 4100) ÷ (line 5100 ÷ 12), from its newest return whose parts add up. Shown only for charities in the default group — not for foundations, small charities, or special cases where the figure misleads.
  • Grants per $100 of assets (foundations) — line 5050 ÷ total assets (line 4200) × 100.
  • Zero and small figures — a line filed as $0 reads “$0 — none filed”. A share worth less than $1 of every $100 reads “less than $1”.

A worked example. Daily Bread Food Bank, year ended Jun 30, 2024: total spending $87,069,775 as filed. Of every $100 it spent, $39 went to its own programs ($34,299,572) and $53 was given to other organizations ($45,810,235); administration took $2 and fundraising $6. Every other page is built by the same arithmetic on that charity's own return.

6. The gates, in plain language

  • The adding-up check. A return's spending lines must add to its totals (within $100 or half a percent). 6,013 charities' newest returns fail that check as published — the failure is in the filings, verified line by line against the CRA's files. Those pages show dollars and say plainly: “the totals in this filing don't add up as published.” Breakdowns are only printed for returns that pass every check — the full split appears on 14,617 pages; the rest show dollars, each with its reason printed.
  • Usable years. A year counts for pooled figures only if it is a normal 12-month year whose return passes the adding-up check. Pooled figures need at least three usable years in the last five.
  • Age. Every page prints its data age. When a charity's newest return is over three years old (8,617 charities), the page shows standing and filing history only.
  • Standing first. A charity that has stopped filing shows that at the top of its page. 29 charities in the CRA's Ontario list have no financial filing in the published data at all; they are named here as non-filers, never dropped. (The CRA's open data carries no live “revoked/suspended” register, so standing here is stated from filing presence and recency — the honest signal the data holds.)
  • Cost-to-raise hold-backs. The pooled figure is shown for 2,591 charities. It is held back when pooled cash donations are under $100,000, when no fundraising costs were filed, when donations are under a quarter of revenue, for foundations and event/lottery charities, and — always — when the pooled figure lands under $1 or over $50 per $100: extremes wait for a person to review them.
  • Zero and negative totals. If a return's totals are zero or negative as filed, its split is shown in dollars only, however well the parts add up — a share of nothing, or of less than nothing, means nothing.

7. Gifts between organizations — the rules

Charities give to each other constantly, and each gift is reported by the giver. BookLook links a gift to the receiving charity's page only when the business number in the giver's return is well-formed, exists in the CRA's list, and the name filed beside it matches a name registered for that number. Of 2,050,336 gift records from Ontario charities, 1,799,376 (87%) pass that test. The other 250,960 — $18,800,373,459 of gifts — are shown on the giver's page exactly as filed, unlinked. A wrong link is worse than no link.

  • Giver pages use the giver's own return only — its newest one — and print the gap: if the recipients it names add to less than its filed total of gifts, the page says how much isn't itemized.
  • Receiver pages never total or reconcile. Each gift is listed from the giver's return, in the giver's year. The receiver's own figure for gifts received is shown separately and never compared — the two come from different returns, in different fiscal years, and aren't expected to match.
  • Nothing is netted and nothing is followed twice. Money a charity passes onward appears on its page and on the receiver's page; BookLook never adds pages together or traces a dollar through a chain.
  • Grants to organizations that are not qualified donees (line 5045) have no published recipient list anywhere — the CRA does not release one — so pages print the filed total and say so.

8. What this site can't tell you

  • Whether a charity's work is effective. A return shows money, not results.
  • Whether a figure is accurate. Returns are published unchecked; only some charities post audited statements (section 2).
  • Anything after a charity's newest published return — usually about two years ago.
  • Who directs a charity, what donors gave individually, or anything the return does not ask.
  • Registration status in real time. The CRA's live list is the authority; this site republishes the open data on a snapshot date.

9. If a figure looks wrong

Write to booklookcanada@proton.me with the charity's name, its business number, and the figure in question. Three different things can be wrong, and each has its own fix:

  • Our mistake — we misread, mis-mapped, or mis-displayed a figure. We fix it, and the fix is entered in the public change log (section 10) with its date.
  • The charity's mistake — the return itself says it wrong. BookLook never edits a filed figure. The charity can file an amended return with the CRA; when the CRA republishes, the page follows. In the meantime, a charity may send a note: notes are published on its page word for word, dated, and labelled “Provided by the charity, not filed with CRA.” Whoever sends one must be reachable through a contact the CRA lists for that charity.
  • The CRA's mistake — the published file itself is wrong. That is the CRA's to correct; we will note it in the change log when a republication changes a figure.

11. Who runs this

BookLook is run by the founder of The Good Guides, a Toronto non-profit that mentors young people in Scarborough, who also builds other civic-data projects. The Good Guides is a registered non-profit organization — it is not a CRA-registered charity: it does not file a T3010 return, and it has no page on this site. No charity can pay to change, hide, or improve its page, and BookLook takes no money from the charities it lists.
